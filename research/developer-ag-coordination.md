# Developer-AG Coordination Patterns in California Housing Enforcement

## Overview

This document maps the pattern of coordination (or aligned action) between private developers, housing advocacy organizations, and the California Attorney General's office in Builder's Remedy and housing enforcement cases. The pattern is relevant to understanding whether the AG's AB 712 notice to Menlo Park regarding 80 Willow Road was the result of independent enforcement discretion or was prompted by the developer or aligned advocacy groups.

## Case Studies

### 1. La Cañada Flintridge: 600 Foothill Boulevard

**The prototype for the developer-AG pincer movement.**

- **Developer:** 600 Foothill Owner LP
- **Developer's law firm:** Holland & Knight (lead attorney: Ryan Leaderman)
- **Companion litigant:** California Housing Defense Fund (CaRLA), a nonprofit that files lawsuits to enforce housing laws
- **AG role:** Intervened on appeal to defend the constitutionality of the Housing Accountability Act (HAA) alongside the developer and CaRLA

**Timeline:**
1. Developer applied for a mixed-use project with affordable housing under Builder's Remedy
2. La Cañada Flintridge denied the application
3. Developer sued (Holland & Knight)
4. CaRLA filed a companion lawsuit
5. Trial court found in developer's favor (Feb 2025, first Builder's Remedy court victory)
6. Court issued writ ordering city to "process the application in accordance with the HAA and state law"
7. City appealed
8. Court required $14 million appeal bond
9. City dismissed its appeal

**Key quote from Holland & Knight:** "Together with the intervention of the Attorney General's office and plaintiff California Housing Defense Fund in their companion case to our litigation, we have helped to set the table to allow more desperately needed housing across the state."

**Significance:** The AG explicitly joined the developer's legal effort. The AG did not independently investigate; the AG backed the developer after the developer sued. This is the template that may have been replicated in the 80 Willow case, but with the AG's new AB 712 notice power replacing the need for intervention in ongoing litigation.

### 2. Huntington Beach: Anti-Builder's Remedy Ordinance

**A state-initiated enforcement action, distinct from developer-prompted enforcement.**

- **Trigger:** Huntington Beach proposed Ordinance 2023-001 banning Builder's Remedy applications locally
- **AG action:** Rob Bonta sent a letter to the City's Planning Commission "in coordination with HCD" urging the city to abandon the ordinance
- **HCD action:** Sent parallel warning letter
- **Developer involvement:** No specific developer at the center; this was a systemic policy enforcement action

**Update (Iteration 15, ~Jul 1, 2026):** San Diego Superior Court granted AG Bonta's anti-SLAPP motion, striking Huntington Beach's cross-complaint against the state. Court found the city's lawsuit challenged the state's protected enforcement of housing law and lacked merit. The state is entitled to recover attorney's fees. AG Bonta: "No more excuses. It's time for the City to adopt and implement a lawful housing element." This ruling establishes precedent that cities cannot counter-sue the AG for enforcing housing law, directly relevant to Menlo Park's options in responding to the AB 712 notice.

**Significance:** This case shows the AG acting proactively to enforce state housing policy without a specific developer complaint. However, the coordination between AG and HCD was explicit and announced. The question for 80 Willow is whether similar coordination occurred with HCD, and whether the AG's action was truly proactive or prompted by the developer.

## The "Pincer Movement" Pattern

Across these cases, a consistent enforcement pattern emerges:

1. **Developer files project** under Builder's Remedy or AB 2011
2. **City resists** (finds inconsistencies, delays, determines ineligibility)
3. **Developer's law firm pressures** (challenged conduct letters, threatened litigation)
4. **Advocacy group adds pressure** (YIMBY Law, CaRLA threat letters or lawsuits)
5. **AG intervenes from above** (legal alerts, AB 712 notices, litigation intervention)
6. **Developer uses AG action as leverage** (press releases, escalated legal threats)

Whether steps 3-6 are coordinated or merely aligned by shared policy goals is the central question our investigation seeks to answer.

### 6. SB 786 and SB 808: Bonta Built the Enforcement Framework, Then Used It

**Research thread added: Iteration 10, Aug 7, 2026**

A critical new dimension to the coordination analysis: AG Bonta did not merely use existing enforcement tools against Menlo Park. He personally sponsored and authored the legislation that expanded those enforcement tools, then deployed them on 80 Willow.

**SB 786 (Sen. Arreguín and AG Bonta, chaptered Oct 10, 2025, Chapter 526)**
- Reforms housing element law litigation
- Resolves conflicts between general plan elements: most recently adopted controls
- Expedites temporary judicial relief in housing element challenges
- Court orders not stayed during appeal (cities cannot delay by appealing)
- HCD can review housing elements for potential decertification when local deadlines are missed
- Source: AG press release, oag.ca.gov, Oct 2025

**SB 808 (AG Bonta, chaptered Oct 10, 2025)**
- Expedites enforcement actions under the Housing Accountability Act (HAA), ministerial approval laws, and ADU laws
- Creates an "expedited writ procedure" for HAA enforcement
- Does not alter substantive law, but dramatically accelerates the timeline for enforcement
- **Specific judicial timelines (from Mondaq analysis, confirmed Aug 17, 2026):**
 - Local agency must certify the administrative record **no later than 15 days** after petition is served
 - Court must set the matter for hearing **no later than 45 days** from the date the petition is filed
 - Court must render decision **30 days after submittal or 75 days after petition filed**, whichever comes first
 - Actions challenging housing disapprovals receive **calendar preference** in both trial court and Court of Appeal
 - Single "out" for courts: presiding judge may appoint a temporary judicial officer if timelines cannot be met
- **Practical impact on 80 Willow:** If N17 files suit after the Oct 27 deadline passes, the entire case could be resolved within ~90 days of filing under SB 808, compared to 1-2+ years under normal litigation timelines. This transforms the developer's leverage: the city cannot run out the clock.
- Source: AG press release, oag.ca.gov, Oct 2025; Mondaq analysis of 2026 housing laws (confirmed via web search Aug 17, 2026)

**The legislative-enforcement pipeline:**
1. Oct 10, 2025: Bonta's SB 786 + SB 808 signed into law, expanding AG enforcement power
2. Jan 1, 2026: AB 712 takes effect, giving AG power to issue notices that trigger $10K/unit fines
3. Mar 2026: Bonta settles with Hollister on housing element compliance ($300K trust fund, compliance requirements)
4. Jul 29, 2026: Bonta issues first AB 712 notice on 80 Willow (unprecedented use)
5. Aug 3, 2026: Developer's attorney sends intent-to-sue using AG notice as leverage
6. Aug 5, 2026: Developer issues press release explicitly leveraging AG's finding

**Key observation:** The AG didn't just enforce existing law. He wrote the fast-track enforcement framework, then deployed it for a specific project owned by entities controlled by the son of a former Russian Energy Minister. The question isn't just whether the developer prompted the AG's action, but whether the AG's entire legislative strategy was designed to create tools that benefit developers who can afford to navigate the enforcement apparatus.

**Hollister settlement contrast:** The Hollister settlement (Mar 2026) targeted a genuinely noncompliant city that hadn't adopted a housing element at all. Menlo Park's housing element IS certified. The AG is using the same tools built for noncompliant cities against a compliant city that is conducting environmental review of a specific project. This overreach is the core of the 80 Willow story.

**Additional insight from Menlo Park project page (updated Aug 5, 2026):**
The city states: "The environmental review work has not begun and is pending the applicant's deposit of funds for the consultant to begin the work." The AG is pressuring the city for not processing the application fast enough, while the developer has not deposited funds for the EIR that the city is required to conduct. The developer is simultaneously claiming the city is obstructing and refusing to fund the city's own review process.

### Critical Distinction for the 80 Willow Investigation

The AG's enforcement against these 5 cities is fundamentally different from the AB 712 notice to Menlo Park:

| Factor | 5-City Actions (Jul 2026) | Menlo Park AB 712 (Jul 29) |
|--------|---------------------------|---------------------------|
| **Legal basis** | Housing Element Law (failure to adopt compliant housing element) | AB 712 / Gov Code § 65914.2 (processing of a specific application) |
| **Housing element status** | Cities do NOT have compliant housing elements | Menlo Park HAS an HCD-approved housing element |
| **Target** | City-wide compliance failure | Single development application (80 Willow) |
| **Enforcement history** | Long-standing AG enforcement pattern (Huntington Beach, Artesia, Malibu, etc.) | First-of-its-kind use of AB 712 against a specific development application |
| **Developer beneficiary** | General public / all potential housing developers | Specific developer (N17/Willow Park LLC/Yusufov) |
| **Process** | HCD refers noncompliant cities after multiple warnings + 30-day response period + two meetings | Unknown process (key PRA question: did the developer prompt the notice?) |
| **Financial exposure** | SB 1037 monthly civil penalties | $10,000/unit = $6.65M (665 units) if city loses post-notice litigation |

**Significance:** The AG simultaneously suing Half Moon Bay (same county) for a straightforward housing element failure while threatening Menlo Park (which is in compliance) over one development application strengthens the argument that the AB 712 notice is qualitatively different from standard enforcement. The 5-city actions follow the established pattern: HCD identifies noncompliance, provides multiple warnings, offers meetings, then refers to AG. The Menlo Park AB 712 notice follows no such documented public process, and the key PRA question remains whether the developer prompted it.

### AB 712 Enforcement Typology: Two Distinct Mechanisms (Aug 13, 2026, updated)

**Critical distinction discovered:** The AG's housing enforcement actions fall into two fundamentally different categories:

**Category 1: Housing Element Compliance Enforcement (existing tools, extensive precedent)**
- Targets: Cities that fail to adopt state-certified housing element plans
- Tools: SB 1037 civil penalties, court-enforced agreements, stipulated judgments, HCD-referred lawsuits
- Precedent: 1,200+ accountability actions, 10 stipulated judgments, 5 lawsuits (Anaheim, Elk Grove, La Canada Flintridge, Norwalk, Huntington Beach), 15 Notices of Violation (Mar 25, 2026), 5 new enforcement actions (Jul 16, 2026: Calexico, Costa Mesa, Half Moon Bay, Ridgecrest, Turlock)
- Process: HCD monitors compliance → gives multiple warnings + meetings → refers to AG for enforcement
- Source: gov.ca.gov/2026/07/16, oag.ca.gov/housing

**Category 2: Project-Specific Processing Enforcement (AB 712 / Gov Code §65914.2, NEW tool)**
- Targets: How a city processes a SPECIFIC development application
- Tool: AB 712 written notice (effective Jan 1, 2026), triggering potential $10K/unit penalties if developer prevails in subsequent litigation
- Precedent: HCD's Mar 23, 2026 NOPV to Beverly Hills (8844 Burton Way) was the first documented AB 712 warning. The AG's Jul 29, 2026 notice to Menlo Park (80 Willow) was the first direct AG use of the mechanism. Both target specific project processing, not housing element compliance.
- Process: Complaint/referral (unknown source) → AG evaluates → sends notice → city has 60 days to correct → developer can then sue with enhanced penalties

**Why this matters for the PRA campaign:**
The 80 Willow AB 712 notice is not just another housing enforcement action in a long line of housing element compliance cases. It is one of the first uses of a brand-new enforcement tool targeting how a city processes a specific project — and the specific project happens to be owned by the son of Russia's former Energy Minister, flagged by Deutsche Bank, and located 5 miles from a NASA/DoD installation.

The AG's office has extensive institutional experience with Category 1 enforcement (housing element compliance). But the 80 Willow notice is Category 2 — a novel use of a law that just became effective. This raises the question: who brought this specific project to the AG's attention, and why did this project merit one of the first uses of the new enforcement tool? PRA-02 targets exactly this question.

## Section 14: AB 712 as First-of-Its-Kind Enforcement Tool (Aug 23, 2026)

**Research thread 5 investigation: Is Menlo Park the first AB 712 notice or part of a pattern?**

### Finding: The 80 Willow AB 712 Notice Appears to Be Among the First Project-Specific Uses of This New Law

AB 712 (Wicks, Chapter 503, Statutes of 2025) took effect January 1, 2026. It created a new enforcement mechanism under Government Code section 65914.2: if the AG or HCD warns a city in writing that its actions violate a "housing reform law," and the city fails to correct the issue within 60 days, then if the applicant subsequently prevails in litigation, the court must impose fines of $10,000 per housing unit (or $50,000 minimum for projects with four or fewer units). For repeat violations, the court must multiply the fine by five.

The AG's July 29, 2026 notice to Menlo Park regarding 80 Willow Road is distinguishable from all other known AG housing enforcement actions in 2025-2026:

### Comparison: AG Housing Enforcement Actions (2024-2026)

| Action | Type | Statute | Target |
|--------|------|---------|--------|
| Huntington Beach (Mar 2023, settled May 2026) | Housing element compliance | SB 1037 | City refused to adopt compliant housing element |
| Artesia (Sep 2025 settlement) | Housing element compliance | SB 1037 | City failed to submit compliant housing element |
| La Habra Heights, Malibu, Fullerton, Coronado, San Bernardino (various 2024-2025) | Housing element compliance | SB 1037 | Various housing element violations |
| Hollister (2024 settlement) | Housing element compliance | SB 1037 | Housing element non-compliance |
| Five cities (Jul 16, 2026: Calexico, Costa Mesa, Half Moon Bay, Ridgecrest, Turlock) | Housing element compliance | SB 1037 | Writ petitions for housing element non-compliance |
| La Canada Flintridge (2024 court order) | Builder's Remedy processing | HAA | Refused to process BR application |
| Goleta (Feb 2025 court order) | SB 330 processing | HAA/SB 330 | Refused to process SB 330 preliminary application |
| **80 Willow / Menlo Park (Jul 29, 2026)** | **Project-specific AB 712 notice** | **AB 712 (Gov't Code 65914.2)** | **City violated AB 2011 and HAA in processing a specific application** |

### Key Distinction

All prior AG enforcement actions fell into two categories:
1. **Housing element compliance** (SB 1037): Cities that failed to adopt or update their housing plans. These are systemic, city-wide failures.
2. **Builder's Remedy / SB 330 legal alerts**: Published guidance documents and amicus briefs in third-party litigation. Not direct enforcement.

The 80 Willow AB 712 notice is the first known instance of the AG using the new AB 712 mechanism to issue a formal written warning regarding a SPECIFIC DEVELOPMENT PROJECT's processing. This is not about whether Menlo Park has a compliant housing element (it does, since March 2024). It's about how the city is processing N17's specific application under AB 2011 and the HAA.

### Implications

1. **Precedent-setting:** If the 80 Willow notice is the first AB 712 project-specific warning, it means the AG chose a Russian-connected developer's 665-unit luxury project as the test case for a brand-new enforcement tool. This raises questions about why this particular project merited the first use of AB 712 rather than, say, an affordable housing project being blocked by a city.

2. **Political timing:** The AB 712 notice was issued July 29, 2026, 13 days after the five-city housing element enforcement wave (July 16). This suggests a two-phase enforcement strategy: first, target systemic non-compliance; then, target project-specific obstruction. But the project chosen for this second phase is controlled by an entity flagged by Deutsche Bank and sanctioned by Ukraine.

3. **Fine exposure:** At $10,000 per housing unit, the 665-unit 80 Willow project would expose Menlo Park to a minimum $6.65 million fine if the AG's warning is not corrected within 60 days AND the developer subsequently prevails in court. For repeat violations in the same housing element cycle, that multiplies to $33.25 million. No other known AB 712 notice has been issued, making Menlo Park's fine exposure unique.

4. **Half Moon Bay parallel:** One of the five cities sued on July 16 (Half Moon Bay) is in the same county as Menlo Park (San Mateo County). The AG is simultaneously enforcing housing law against two San Mateo County cities through different mechanisms. This could reflect the AG's view of San Mateo County as a particularly resistant jurisdiction, or it could reflect the political pressure from housing advocacy groups concentrated in the Bay Area.

5. **PRA-01 relevance:** This finding strengthens the case for PRA-01 (AG Communications). If 80 Willow is the first AB 712 project-specific notice, any communications between the developer/YIMBY Law and the AG's office that precipitated this notice are of heightened public interest. The question becomes: did the AG independently identify Menlo Park's processing of 80 Willow as a violation, or was the AG prompted by the developer or an aligned organization?

#### The 80 Willow Distinction
The Menlo Park AB 712 notice (Jul 29, 2026) is fundamentally different:
- **Menlo Park HAS a certified housing element** (approved by HCD March 21, 2024)
- The notice targets how the city is **processing a specific application** — not whether it has planned for housing

#### Analytical Significance
The enforcement pattern reveals a two-tier system:
1. **Housing element enforcement** (systemic): Against cities that refuse to plan for housing. These are broadly supported — even NIMBY-skeptic observers agree cities should plan.
2. **Project-specific enforcement** (targeted): Against a city with a certified housing element, on behalf of a specific developer. The 80 Willow notice is the only known case in this category.

This distinction is critical for the PRA requests (understanding what communications preceded the notice) and for the articles (framing the AG's enforcement as qualitatively different from other housing actions).
