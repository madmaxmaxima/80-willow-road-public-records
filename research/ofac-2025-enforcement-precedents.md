# OFAC 2025 Enforcement Precedents: Russian-Linked Real Estate

## Purpose

Documenting 2025 OFAC enforcement actions that directly parallel the Yusufov/Willow Project LLC ownership structure, strengthening the case for CFIUS referral and congressional outreach.

---

## Overview: 2025 Was OFAC's Most Aggressive Year for Russia-Related Real Estate Enforcement

According to Sidley Austin's annual review (Feb 2026), OFAC announced 14 enforcement actions in 2025 with total penalties exceeding $265 million (vs $49M in 2024, a 5.4x increase). Eight of the 14 actions (57%) were Russia-related. Three actions targeted individuals. OFAC's substantive priorities were: (1) continued Russia sanctions enforcement, (2) holding advisers and intermediaries accountable as "gatekeepers," and (3) rejecting overly formalistic ownership structures.

Source: Sidley Austin LLP, "Five Key Takeaways From 2025 U.S. Sanctions Enforcement," Feb 2026.

## Case 1: King Holdings / "U.S. Person-1" ($4.7M penalty, Nov 24, 2025)

### Facts
- An individual, acting through Atlanta-based real estate investment company King Holdings LLC, acquired through foreclosure a residential property owned by a person blocked under OFAC Russia sanctions.
- Despite public notice of the sanctioned individual's property interest, the investor purchased, mortgaged ($872K, Apr 2023), renovated, and listed the property (Aug 2023).
- On Feb 1, 2024, OFAC issued a cease-and-desist order and administrative subpoena.
- The investor certified the subpoena response as complete but omitted the pending sale.
- On Mar 5, 2024, King Holdings certified compliance with the cease-and-desist through counsel.
- Eight days later, the sale closed for $1.4M to an unwitting third-party buyer.

### Penalty
- $4,677,552, the statutory maximum and the largest OFAC penalty against an individual in a public enforcement action.
- Egregious case. Not voluntarily self-disclosed.

### Aggravating Factors (Directly Quoted from OFAC)
1. "Acted willfully by dealing in the blocked property for nearly a year after receiving clear and actual notice from OFAC."
2. "Willfully certified the accuracy and completeness of a subpoena response that was inaccurate and incomplete."
3. Year-long dealings constituted a "pattern of violative conduct."
4. Knowledge "at all times" of the violations.
5. "Exposed multiple third parties to potential economic harm and legal liability and significantly damaged the integrity of OFAC's sanctions."
6. "Failed to cooperate with OFAC's investigation."

### Parallel to 80 Willow
- Property owned through an LLC by a person connected to a blocked/sanctioned Russian party.
- Sale was processed despite bank compliance flag (Deutsche Bank SAR in the Yusufov case; public SDN notice in King Holdings).
- Third parties (lenders, attorneys, unwitting buyers) exposed to legal liability, mirroring the way Menlo Park taxpayers and city officials are being exposed to liability through the AG's enforcement action benefiting Willow Project LLC.
- OFAC imposed the MAXIMUM penalty for real estate dealings linked to sanctioned Russians.

Source: Treasury press release sb0323, Nov 24, 2025; VinciWorks analysis, Jan 2026; Lexology/Mayer Brown analysis, Nov 2025.

## Case 2: Gracetown, Inc. ($7.1M penalty, Dec 4, 2025)

### Facts
- Gracetown, Inc., a New York property management firm, processed 24 payments totaling $31,250 for a BVI entity ultimately owned by sanctioned Russian oligarch Oleg Deripaska.
- Deripaska was designated on the SDN list on April 6, 2018 for acting as/on behalf of a senior Russian government official and for operating in the Russian energy sector.
- Gracetown received explicit notice from OFAC in April 2018 but continued processing transactions and failed to block or report them for over 45 months.

### Penalty
- $7,139,305 (slightly reduced from statutory maximum of $8,906,358).
- Egregious case. Not voluntarily self-disclosed.

### Key Factors
1. Willful conduct: continued dealings after explicit OFAC notice.
2. Deficient remediation: blocked and reported transactions only belatedly.
3. Mitigating: eventual cooperation and tolling agreement.

### Parallel to 80 Willow
- Property management firm (gatekeeper) dealing with entities owned by a sanctioned Russian oligarch designated for government connections and energy sector involvement.
- Igor Yusufov was Russia's Energy Minister (2001-2004) and served on Rosneft/Gazprom boards. If designated, any intermediary (Canopic Advisory, Allrise Capital, N17 Development) managing or facilitating transactions with Willow Project LLC property would face exactly this type of enforcement.
- The $7.1M penalty for $31,250 in transactions (228x the transaction value) shows OFAC's willingness to impose penalties vastly exceeding the economic value of the violations.
- OFAC's focus on property management firms as gatekeepers directly implicates Canopic Advisory's role managing Willow Project LLC.

Source: OFAC Enforcement Release 20251204_33; Lewis Baach Kaufmann Middlemiss PLLC analysis, Dec 2025.

## Case 3: Unnamed Lawyer/Fiduciary ($1.09M settlement, Dec 9, 2025)

### Facts
- An individual, a lawyer and former U.S. government official, provided fiduciary services to a trust affiliated with a sanctioned Russian oligarch.
- The individual had received legal advice that the trust was not blocked property.
- However, the SDN retained control over trust decisions through a family member who acted as a proxy in investment-related matters.
- OFAC stated the proxy relationship "indicated that [the SDN] maintained a property interest in the trust."
- The legal advice was predicated on the "apparently false understanding" that the proxy had no substantive management role.

### Penalty
- $1,092,000 settlement.

### Parallel to 80 Willow (STRONGEST PRECEDENT)
- This is the father-son proxy pattern. The SDN (parent) retained control through a family member (proxy). OFAC found this meant the SDN had a property interest.
- Igor Yusufov (sanctioned by Ukraine, documented Putin Energy Minister, Gazprom/Rosneft board) is Vitaly Yusufov's father. The $72M purchase of 80 Willow Road is managed through Willow Project LLC. Deutsche Bank's compliance team flagged the transaction specifically because of Igor Yusufov's political connections.
- OFAC's finding that "the SDN retained control over decisions through a family member" could apply directly to the Yusufov ownership structure if OFAC were to investigate whether Igor Yusufov has any role in directing, advising, or benefiting from Vitaly's U.S. real estate investments.
- The fact that professional legal advice failed to protect the fiduciary underscores that legal opinions about the Yusufov structure being "clean" may not withstand OFAC scrutiny.
- Key quote from Sidley Austin analysis: "The status of an entity or corporate structure is only the first line of inquiry and needs to be immediately followed by an analysis of what touchpoints, direct and indirect, the SDN or blocked person has to the contemplated activity and what interest, however remote or tangential, the SDN or blocked person has in the assets."

Source: OFAC Enforcement Release, Dec 9, 2025; Sidley Austin analysis, Feb 2026.

## Key Enforcement Themes from 2025 (Per Sidley Austin)

### 1. "Form Over Substance" Rejection
OFAC now explicitly rejects reliance on corporate formalities to distance transactions from sanctioned persons. The 50% Rule is "only the first line of inquiry" and must be "immediately followed by an analysis of what touchpoints, direct and indirect, the SDN or blocked person has to the contemplated activity and what interest, however remote or tangential, the SDN or blocked person has."

**Application to 80 Willow:** Even if Vitaly Yusufov is not on the SDN list and Igor Yusufov's ownership stake in Willow Project LLC is formally below 50%, OFAC's 2025 enforcement posture means that any touchpoint between Igor and the property (financial backing, advisory role, beneficial interest) could trigger a violation.

### 2. Gatekeepers as Primary Targets
OFAC views "advisers and intermediaries as essential participants whose conduct can either prevent or enable sanctions violations." The GVA Capital action ($215M+ penalty) specifically targeted an investment advisory firm for "knowingly manag[ing] or deploy[ing] capital connected to sanctioned persons."

**Application to 80 Willow:** Canopic Advisory (Garrett Camporine) manages Willow Project LLC. Allrise Capital (Ruslan Zinurov, Mikhail Trubchik) finances N17 Development projects. If either entity has knowledge of Igor Yusufov's interest in the property or its proceeds, they face gatekeeper liability.

### 3. Individual Liability Is Increasing
Three of 14 enforcement actions targeted individuals, all in Russia-related cases. OFAC is moving beyond entity-level penalties.

**Application to 80 Willow:** Oisin Heneghan (N17 Development), Garrett Camporine (Canopic Advisory), and any attorney facilitating the development could face individual liability if they know of or should know of Igor Yusufov's connection.
